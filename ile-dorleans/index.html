Sainte‑Anne‑de‑Beaupré, then Île d'Orléans

A full-day slow-travel loop from Québec City, departing around 10:30.
Planned for five — built so the grandmothers never walk far, the basilica interior is guaranteed, and the wineries end the day with no clock on them.

Why the basilica comes first

The Sanctuaire interior closes at 4:00 PM on Mondays. Leaving at 10:30, going to the island first would put you there after closing — so the order flips: drive straight to the Sanctuaire (~35 min on Route 138), be inside before noon, then cross to the island with no deadline for the rest of the day.

Bonus: the island churches are more likely open in the afternoon, and Cassis serves until 9:00 PM — the last stop can be dinner.

The route

From Québec City, Route 138 northeast to the Sanctuaire first. Back along 138 and across the bridge, then one road — Route 368, Chemin Royal — circles the island, about 67 km: the Sainte-Pétronille tip first, the south-shore villages out to the eastern point, then the north shore back so the wineries land last. Home across the bridge whenever you're done.

Order: Sanctuaire · then Sainte-Pétronille · Saint-Laurent · Saint-Jean · Saint-François · Sainte-Famille · Saint-Pierre.

1

Sanctuaire Sainte-Anne-de-Beaupré mainland first · ~35 min from Québec City

One of North America's great pilgrimage basilicas (pilgrims since 1658), neo-Romanesque, with glowing ceiling mosaics, stained glass, and stone and wood carving. Arriving before noon means the interior is guaranteed.

Do: tour the main floor — fully wheelchair and mobility friendly. Shrine store and information centre on site.

Mon: basilica 9:00 AM – 4:00 PM · store 9:30 – 4:00Access: fully accessible for reduced mobility (main floor)
Maps Website
The story

Pilgrims have come to Sainte-Anne-de-Beaupré since 1658, and the present neo-Romanesque basilica is one of the great pilgrimage churches of North America. Inside are luminous ceiling mosaics, stained glass, and stone and wood carving, and the main floor is level and wheelchair friendly. It sits about 25 to 30 minutes northeast of the island bridge on Route 138.

2

Sainte-Pétronille island SW tip · belvedere

First village after the bridge and the classic opening view — from the point you look straight across the water to Montmorency Falls and the Québec City skyline.

Do: roll slowly through the tidy Victorian summer streets, then pause at the lookout. The view works from the car or a few flat steps.

Mon: village & lookout open anytimeAccess: very easy — roadside and flat
Maps
The story

Huron families fleeing Iroquois raids built a fort near here in 1651, at the spot still called l'Anse du Fort. In the 1800s the village became a fashionable summer retreat for Québec City society, and the landscape painter Horatio Walker kept his studio here. If you want one more tasting, the Vignoble de Sainte-Pétronille sits right at the tip with the same falls-and-city view.

3

Chocolaterie de l'Île d'Orléans Sainte-Pétronille

Beloved island chocolate maker in a period home. Chocolates plus an ice cream and gelato window.

Do: pick up maple and blackcurrant chocolates or a cone, and rest on the patio.

Mon: open daily in summer, ~9:00 AM into the evening — confirm day-ofAccess: easy — flat, patio seating
Maps Website
The story

A family chocolate maker working out of a period island home with Belgian and French couverture. The blackcurrant and maple pieces nod to two island traditions — cassis and the sugar shack — and the ice cream window is a favorite on a warm day.

4

Saint-Laurent south shore · maritime village

The island's old boat-building village, low-key and easy on the legs. The Parc maritime keeps the story of the chalouperies — the workshops that once built the island's rowboats — and the marina gives a pretty harbour pause.

Do: a slow drive through, a short flat stroll at the marina or Parc maritime, photos of the fleet.

Mon: village anytime · Parc maritime grounds in seasonAccess: easy — flat, as short as you like
Maps
The story

Saint-Laurent was the island's boat-building centre — its chalouperies supplied rowboats and small craft to the whole St. Lawrence shore. The Parc maritime sits on the site of the last working boatyard, and the marina below the church is one of the prettiest small harbours on the river.

5

Saint-Jean south shore · waterfront promenade

A flat waterfront promenade along the river wall, a handsome church square, and the café La Boulange for coffee, bread, or a light lunch.

Do: stroll the promenade as far as legs like, then a café stop at La Boulange if it's lunchtime.

Mon: promenade anytime · café hours varyAccess: easy — flat, benches along the water
Maps
The story

Saint-Jean was the village of the river pilots — the men who guided ships through the St. Lawrence channel — and their prosperous stone houses still line the road. The churchyard sits right on the water, and the promenade along the sea wall is the easiest waterfront walk on the island.

6

Manoir Mauvide-Genest Saint-Jean · south shore

A 1734 seigneurial manor, a National Historic Site, and the anchor of the south shore.

Do: audio or self-guided tour of three floors of period rooms, the apothecary garden, and a riverfront park. The 80-year-old can relax in the flat front garden with river views while others go upstairs.

Mon: open 7 days, 10:00 AM – 5:00 PM (2026 season confirmed)Access: Kéroul-certified · grounds flat, interior has stairs
Maps Website
The story

Built in 1734 for Jean Mauvide, a ship's surgeon who rose to become seigneur of the southern half of the island, and his wife Marie-Anne Genest. It is one of the very few island buildings spared when General Wolfe's troops burned much of Île d'Orléans in the summer of 1759, which makes it one of the best-preserved seigneurial manors of New France. Restored around 2000, it now runs as an accredited museum with period rooms, an apothecary and heritage garden, and a riverfront park.

7

Saint-François Observation Tower eastern point

The island's far tip, where the river broadens and freshwater meets the tidal estuary. A roadside wooden tower gives wide water views and passing ships.

Do: climbers take the stairs for a 360° view; everyone else gets the same water panorama from flat ground. Quick photo stop.

Mon: open-air, daytimeAccess: easy at ground level · tower stairs optional
Maps
The story

Saint-François is the quiet far end of the island, where the St. Lawrence widens and its freshwater begins to mix with the tidal estuary. The roadside wooden tower gives a big sweep of water, ships, and the south-shore hills.

8

Sainte-Famille Church north shore

The island's oldest parish (1661) and one of Québec's most photographed rural churches, with a rare triple bell tower (built 1743–1747). Passing in the afternoon means the interior is more likely open.

Do: photos from the flat churchyard; step inside if the door is open. Heritage houses sit across the road if you want more.

Mon: grounds anytime · interior often open summer afternoonsAccess: easy — flat, short
Maps
The story

Sainte-Famille was the island's first parish, founded in 1661, which makes this the oldest community on Île d'Orléans. The present church was built between 1743 and 1747, and its triple bell tower and twin front turrets are unique among Québec's rural churches. Two heritage houses across the road — Maison de nos Aieux and Maison Drouin — tell the story of the founding families. The oldest church building on the island is actually the Église de Saint-Pierre of 1717, the oldest rural church in New France, which you pass near the wineries. Félix Leclerc, the beloved Québécois songwriter, is buried at Saint-Pierre, with the Espace Félix-Leclerc museum nearby.

9

Vignoble Isle de Bacchus Saint-Pierre

One of Québec's pioneering wineries (since 1982), named for Jacques Cartier's 1535 name for the island, Isle de Bacchus.

Do: taste the cool-climate whites and reds, share a charcuterie and cheese board in the bistro, and stroll the vine terrace above the river if legs allow. Arriving late afternoon — call ahead to confirm tasting hours.

Mon: open daily in summer — confirm hours by phoneAccess: easy · boutique and bistro seating · vine walk optional
Maps Call (418) 828-9562 Website
The story

When Jacques Cartier reached the island in 1535 he first named it Isle de Bacchus for the wild grapevines covering it. This estate, started in 1982, was a pioneer of modern Québec winemaking and took back that old name. Expect crisp cool-climate whites and reds, a bistro with Québec charcuterie and cheese, and rows of vines on a terrace above the river.

10

Cassis Monna & Filles Saint-Pierre · dinner finale

The famous blackcurrant estate and économusée by the bridge — the grand finale. The terrace looks right at the Île d'Orléans bridge, and the kitchen serves until 9:00 PM, so this is dinner.

Do: taste crème de cassis and black liqueurs, browse the gourmet shop, dinner or a drink at La Monnaguette, and the blackcurrant-vanilla soft serve at the dairy bar.

Mon: 10:00 AM – 9:00 PM, open all week (confirmed)Access: very easy — flat, plenty of seating, river views
Maps Website
The story

The Monna family has grown blackcurrants here for more than 35 years and turned the little fruit into crème de cassis, liqueurs, and spirits on a 22-hectare estate. It is a recognized économusée — a working artisan producer you can visit. The riverside terrace faces the Île d'Orléans bridge, the gourmet shop is full of blackcurrant everything, and the dairy bar's blackcurrant-vanilla soft serve is the reason people pull over.

Practical notes

Monday. Every anchor stop — the Sanctuaire interior (before 4:00), the drive and views, Chocolaterie, Manoir, and both wineries — is open Monday. Some small island boutiques and museums close Mondays, so the plan leans on places confirmed open.

Lunch. Flexible by design: a cone at the Chocolaterie, a café lunch at La Boulange in Saint-Jean, or a board at the Isle de Bacchus bistro — then dinner at Cassis to finish.

Monasteries. There is no monastery on the island itself. Its spiritual heritage is the historic churches, and the Sanctuaire — now first — is the spiritual anchor of the day.

Food and rest. La Monnaguette at Cassis and the bistro at Isle de Bacchus are the best sit-down options, both with easy seating. The Manoir, both wineries, and the Sanctuaire all have restrooms and level parking near the door, which helps with the group.
