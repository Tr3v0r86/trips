Île d'Orléans, then Sainte‑Anne‑de‑Beaupré

A full-day slow-travel driving loop from Québec City.
A relaxed Monday in summer 2026, planned for five — built so the grandmothers never walk far, the wineries come last on the island, and the day can end inside the basilica.

Read this first: the 4:00 PM basilica cut-off

The Sanctuaire basilica interior closes at 4:00 PM on Mondays. To end the day inside it, arrive by about 3:15 — which means leaving the last winery by roughly 3:00 and starting the loop around 9:00 AM.

Two ways to play it. Make the interior: keep the winery visits to a midday lunch and reach the Sanctuaire by 3:15. Or linger at the wineries into the late afternoon — you can still walk the Sanctuaire grounds and see the exterior, but the interior will be closed.

The route

One road, Route 368 (Chemin Royal), circles the island — about 67 km of loop. From Québec City you cross the bridge, head to the Sainte-Pétronille tip first, follow the south shore out to the eastern point, then come back along the north shore so you finish at the Saint-Pierre wineries. Then cross the bridge and drive northeast on Route 138 to the Sanctuaire.

Village order: Sainte-Pétronille · Saint-Laurent · Saint-Jean · Saint-François · Sainte-Famille · Saint-Pierre · then off-island to Sainte-Anne-de-Beaupré.

The stops

Every Maps button opens the stop for driving directions. Hours are for Monday, summer 2026.

1

Sainte-Pétronille SW tip · belvedere

First village after the bridge and the classic opening view — from the point you look straight across the water to Montmorency Falls and the Québec City skyline.

Do: roll slowly through the tidy Victorian summer streets, then pause at the lookout. The view works from the car or a few flat steps.

Mon: village & lookout open anytimeAccess: very easy — roadside and flat
Maps
The story

Huron families fleeing Iroquois raids built a fort near here in 1651, at the spot still called l'Anse du Fort. In the 1800s the village became a fashionable summer retreat for Québec City society, and the landscape painter Horatio Walker kept his studio here. If you want one more tasting, the Vignoble de Sainte-Pétronille sits right at the tip with the same falls-and-city view.

2

Chocolaterie de l'Île d'Orléans Sainte-Pétronille

Beloved island chocolate maker in a period home. Chocolates plus an ice cream and gelato window.

Do: pick up maple and blackcurrant chocolates or a cone, and rest on the patio.

Mon: open daily in summer, ~9:00 AM into the evening — confirm day-ofAccess: easy — flat, patio seating
Maps Website
The story

A family chocolate maker working out of a period island home with Belgian and French couverture. The blackcurrant and maple pieces nod to two island traditions — cassis and the sugar shack — and the ice cream window is a favorite on a warm day.

3

Manoir Mauvide-Genest Saint-Jean · south shore

A 1734 seigneurial manor, a National Historic Site, and the anchor of the south shore.

Do: audio or self-guided tour of three floors of period rooms, the apothecary garden, and a riverfront park. The 80-year-old can relax in the flat front garden with river views while others go upstairs.

Mon: open 7 days, 10:00 AM – 5:00 PM (2026 season confirmed)Access: Kéroul-certified · grounds flat, interior has stairs
Maps Website
The story

Built in 1734 for Jean Mauvide, a ship's surgeon who rose to become seigneur of the southern half of the island, and his wife Marie-Anne Genest. It is one of the very few island buildings spared when General Wolfe's troops burned much of Île d'Orléans in the summer of 1759, which makes it one of the best-preserved seigneurial manors of New France. Restored around 2000, it now runs as an accredited museum with period rooms, an apothecary and heritage garden, and a riverfront park.

4

Saint-François Observation Tower eastern point

The island's far tip, where the river broadens and freshwater meets the tidal estuary. A roadside wooden tower gives wide water views and passing ships.

Do: climbers take the stairs for a 360° view; everyone else gets the same water panorama from flat ground. Quick photo stop.

Mon: open-air, daytimeAccess: easy at ground level · tower stairs optional
Maps
The story

Saint-François is the quiet far end of the island, where the St. Lawrence widens and its freshwater begins to mix with the tidal estuary. The roadside wooden tower gives a big sweep of water, ships, and the south-shore hills.

5

Sainte-Famille Church north shore

The island's oldest parish (1661) and one of Québec's most photographed rural churches, with a rare triple bell tower (built 1743–1747).

Do: photos from the flat churchyard; the interior is often open on summer afternoons. Heritage houses sit across the road if you want more.

Mon: grounds anytime · interior varies (summer afternoons)Access: easy — flat, short
Maps
The story

Sainte-Famille was the island's first parish, founded in 1661, which makes this the oldest community on Île d'Orléans. The present church was built between 1743 and 1747, and its triple bell tower and twin front turrets are unique among Québec's rural churches. Two heritage houses across the road — Maison de nos Aieux and Maison Drouin — tell the story of the founding families. The oldest church building on the island is actually the Église de Saint-Pierre of 1717, the oldest rural church in New France, which you pass near the wineries. Félix Leclerc, the beloved Québécois songwriter, is buried at Saint-Pierre, with the Espace Félix-Leclerc museum nearby.

6

Vignoble Isle de Bacchus Saint-Pierre

One of Québec's pioneering wineries (since 1982), named for Jacques Cartier's 1535 name for the island, Isle de Bacchus.

Do: taste the cool-climate whites and reds, share a charcuterie and cheese board in the bistro, and stroll the vine terrace above the river if legs allow.

Mon: open daily in summer — confirm hours by phoneAccess: easy · boutique and bistro seating · vine walk optional
Maps Call (418) 828-9562 Website
The story

When Jacques Cartier reached the island in 1535 he first named it Isle de Bacchus for the wild grapevines covering it. This estate, started in 1982, was a pioneer of modern Québec winemaking and took back that old name. Expect crisp cool-climate whites and reds, a bistro with Québec charcuterie and cheese, and rows of vines on a terrace above the river.

7

Cassis Monna & Filles Saint-Pierre · last island stop

The famous blackcurrant estate and économusée by the bridge — your grand finale on the island. The terrace looks right at the Île d'Orléans bridge.

Do: taste crème de cassis and black liqueurs, browse the gourmet shop, have lunch or a drink at La Monnaguette, and try the blackcurrant-vanilla soft serve at the dairy bar.

Mon: 10:00 AM – 9:00 PM, open all week (confirmed)Access: very easy — flat, plenty of seating, river views
Maps Website
The story

The Monna family has grown blackcurrants here for more than 35 years and turned the little fruit into crème de cassis, liqueurs, and spirits on a 22-hectare estate. It is a recognized économusée — a working artisan producer you can visit. The riverside terrace faces the Île d'Orléans bridge, the gourmet shop is full of blackcurrant everything, and the dairy bar's blackcurrant-vanilla soft serve is the reason people pull over.

★

Finale: Sanctuaire Sainte-Anne-de-Beaupré mainland · ~25–30 min NE of the bridge

One of North America's great pilgrimage basilicas (pilgrims since 1658), neo-Romanesque, with glowing ceiling mosaics, stained glass, and stone and wood carving.

Do: tour the main floor — fully wheelchair and mobility friendly. Shrine store and information centre on site.

Mon: basilica 9:00 AM – 4:00 PM · store 9:30 – 4:00 · grounds accessible outside those hoursAccess: fully accessible for reduced mobility (main floor)
Maps Website
The story

Pilgrims have come to Sainte-Anne-de-Beaupré since 1658, and the present neo-Romanesque basilica is one of the great pilgrimage churches of North America. Inside are luminous ceiling mosaics, stained glass, and stone and wood carving, and the main floor is level and wheelchair friendly. It sits about 25 to 30 minutes northeast of the bridge on Route 138. Remember the Monday interior closing time of 4:00 PM.

Practical notes

Monday. Your anchor stops — the drive and views, Chocolaterie, Manoir, both wineries, and the Sanctuaire interior — are open Monday. Some small island boutiques and museums close Mondays, so the plan leans on places confirmed open.

Cute villages if you have time. Saint-Jean has a flat waterfront promenade and the café La Boulange. Saint-Laurent has the Parc maritime and marina. Both are low-key and easy on the legs.

Monasteries. There is no monastery on the island itself. Its spiritual heritage is the historic churches above, and the Sanctuaire is the grand finale.

Food and rest. La Monnaguette at Cassis and the bistro at Isle de Bacchus are your best sit-down options, both with easy seating. The Manoir, both wineries, and the Sanctuaire all have restrooms and level parking near the door, which helps with the group.
